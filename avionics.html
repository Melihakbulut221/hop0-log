Flight software

A C port that matches the Python reference to about 10-6 step by step. It passes static analysis (gcc analyzer, clang-tidy, cppcheck with MISRA C:2012), runs fuzzing under ASan and UBSan, and runs on a Cortex-M7 target in QEMU with the same outputs.

It runs at 50 Hz on the flight computer: it reads every sensor, estimates the state with a multi-rate navigation filter (50 Hz IMU, sparse and delayed GNSS, radar altimeter), and commands the engine, the gimbal and the RCS. Guidance, navigation and control: a classical controller with a multi-rate navigation filter, plus a predictive terminal law. An estimate from instruction counts in emulation puts the worst frame at 0.7–1.4% of the 20 ms frame, assuming 1–2 cycles per instruction; it has not been measured on the board yet.

Software-in-the-loop

What the loop is, and what it is not. The flight code is the real C code, but today it runs on a PC, not on the flight computer: that is software-in-the-loop (SIL). The same code also runs on an emulated Cortex-M7 in QEMU and gives the same outputs. Hardware-in-the-loop, with the code on the FC-0 board and the board's own Ethernet port in the loop, starts when the board exists; the protocol is already the one the board will speak. Until 2026-11-13 this site called the PC loop "HIL"; that overstated it, and every such number is now marked SIL.

The C flight software runs as a separate process and talks to the simulator over a UDP protocol with timestamps, CRC, injected latency, packet loss and sensor faults. Every landing number on this site marked SIL comes from this loop, run in lockstep at 50 Hz.

An in-process emulator of the SIL server, with the same random streams, latency, packet-loss and command-horizon logic, reproduces SIL episode by episode (300 of 300 identical in the deterministic scenarios). Candidate changes are screened there and then confirmed in SIL on other seeds. Why that mattered is on Landing: trusting the right simulator.

FC-0 flight computer

Runs the flight software at 50 Hz: reads every sensor, estimates the state, and commands the engine, the gimbal and the RCS.

Routed · not ready to fabricate
  • STM32H723 Cortex-M7 with a double-precision FPU. An estimate from instruction counts in emulation puts the flight software at 0.7–1.4% of each 20 ms frame (not yet measured on the board).
  • IMU on the board, a place for an external tactical IMU, RTK GNSS, barometer.
  • Three CAN-FD buses: propulsion, gimbal and RCS, auxiliary.
  • 16 MB flash logs the whole flight; FRAM keeps the last records through a power loss.
  • An external watchdog and an isolated flight-termination interface that do not depend on the software.
  • An arm vote to the engine controller through two series stages, each gated in hardware by the watchdog and the reset line: a hung or rebooting processor withdraws it, and an open wire reads as safe.
  • 318 parts. Schematic passes ERC; every pin checked against the vendor's pin data. 23 connections left to finish by hand after autorouting.

    150 × 110 mm, six layers. Signal connectors along the top and bottom, valve power on the right edge.

    ECU-1 engine controller

    Sequences the engine: opens the valves, fires the igniter, reads chamber and feed pressures and temperatures, and commands both pump speeds.

    Routed · not ready to fabricate
    • STM32G474. Ten pressure, four thermocouple, two RTD and four valve-position inputs.
    • Eight peak-and-hold valve and igniter channels, plus a current-controlled proportional valve.
    • A hardware arming chain: the valves get power only if the arm plug, the termination unit, the flight computer, the watchdog and the controller all agree.
    • The main fuel valve is interlocked in hardware to proof of ignition.
    • Reads the power-distribution unit's pre-charge, contactor and fault-latch status as active-low opto-coupled lines, so an open harness or an unpowered unit reads as not ready or faulted; bus voltage, pack voltage and bus current come in as analog monitors.
    • Its sensor inputs, valve-position switches, inverter enable, fault and supply lines, the power-distribution status lines and the arming discretes survive a sustained short to the vehicle battery or to a reversed battery.
    • Each valve and igniter output has a hardware over-current trip: a fast comparator on the sense resistor switches the channel off in an estimated fraction of a microsecond, and the outputs are also gated by the hardware arming chain, so firmware alone cannot energise a valve.
    • Each valve output now has a 1 µH series inductor between its switch and the connector, so even a short right at the connector sees a minimum loop inductance. A simulation that swept the harness inductance from 0 to 2 µH had shown the earlier circuit could exceed the switch's voltage rating on a very short wire; with the inductor, the switch stays below 80 % of its rating across that range. The figures rest on estimated component values.
    • After a trip, a slow shared retry clock re-arms the channel every 0.2 to 0.4 ms instead of on every PWM edge, which in the thermal estimate keeps the clamp diode within its limit during a sustained short, for harness inductance up to 2 µH. If the clock stops, a tripped channel stays off. A valve wire shorted to ground is still not covered.
    • The data-bus protection was replaced with parts rated for a sustained short to the 36 V battery; the bus termination resistors would still burn open in that case.
    • A shorted sensor wire can fake a hardware permit, so before ignition the firmware requires every ignition-proof and chamber-pressure comparator to read idle, or it will not arm.
    • 672 parts, ERC clean. The switched valve supply now runs on dedicated internal copper instead of narrow routed tracks, and the routed layout passes the design-rule check with no open connections. It is not ready for fabrication: spacing on the 36 V nets, the copper stack-up and several long repair tracks still need review.

      150 × 105 mm, four layers of heavy copper on a cooling plate. Three phase legs across the top, logic along the bottom.

      INV-1 pump inverter

      Turns the pump battery into three-phase current for one pump motor. There are two, identical.

      Short detection in each leg, calculated only · routed, not yet reproducibly · not ready to fabricate
      • 150 V MOSFETs, two in parallel per switch. A full 24S pack is 100.8 V, not the nominal 80 V, already above the rating of 100 V switches before regenerative braking adds a few volts.
      • The inverter now carries the damper that the capacitor-board analysis asked for: twenty-four ceramic capacitors in series with twelve low-inductance power resistors, split across the three legs between the positive bus and the low-side return.
      • The resonance between the inverter's ceramic capacitors and the film bank was re-derived independently and checked against the capacitor-board model. With the damper and the two bus studs moved to 12 mm apart, the peak impedance drops about tenfold, and the gate driver's high-side offset pin (VS) stays about 4 V below the driver's recommended limit with nominal capacitor values, across the range of inverter-side wiring inductance that was swept.
      • The damper was sized for the real stud height under the capacitor board; its resistors keep a margin of about 40 % against their derated rating in the worst case.
      • A ceramic damper was chosen over a film capacitor: the film damps slightly better, but the ceramic version weighs less than a quarter as much and avoids a tall leaded part on a board that has to survive launch vibration.
      • All sixty DC-link capacitors now sit in rows directly above the high-side switches, on both sides of the board, within 10 mm of the switch tabs and over the stacked supply and return planes. The estimated commutation-loop inductance fell by about a sixth; it is an analytic estimate that still needs a double-pulse measurement.
      • The 12 V supply, enable and fault lines of the engine-controller harness now withstand a sustained short to the vehicle battery in either polarity and the standard automotive supply pulses, with the same criterion as the engine controller. An overvoltage switch protects the gate driver's 20 V supply pins; after a pulse trips it, a hold-up capacitor bank keeps the supply above the driver's undervoltage limit through the restart, in the calculation for the worst-case load and outage; this ride-through still has to be confirmed on hardware. The enable input uses a logic-level transistor that keeps more than 1 V of margin at -40 C.
      • Every gate resistor, turn-off diode and gate pull-down now sits within about 6 mm of its switch's gate pad, in channels between and beside the paired switches; before, they were 20 to 50 mm away.
      • 468 parts (the 24 film capacitors are counted on the capacitor board), no ERC errors; the pin assignment check against the vendor's open pin data still passes. The fault output has a series resistor sized together with the engine controller's pull-up, so a sustained short on that line is limited in both polarities.
      • The gate-drive connections are drawn by the placement script and checked before the signal side is auto-routed, with separate clearance rules for the bus, the three floating high-side gate-drive domains and each harness line. One automated run from a clean copy reached every connection with no rule violations, and the routed board matches the current schematic part for part. A repeat run on the same inputs did not, so the routing is not yet reproducible.
      • The inverter's three-phase gate driver went out of production, and the planned replacement family of half-bridge drivers turned out to be end-of-life as well. The board now uses one 180 V half-bridge driver per leg from another supplier, placed between the two paired switches of that leg, which cuts the driver-to-gate distance to about a third. No second source with the same footprint was found in this voltage class, so the driver is single-sourced for now.
      • The protection the old driver had built in is rebuilt from small parts that set one latch blocking all six gate inputs. The latch comes up set at power-on and is cleared only by the controller after a new enable command. A separate supply-voltage lockout stops switching before the gate drive becomes too weak; the new driver's own lockout sits too low for these switches.
      • Each leg's low-side current shunt, which sits inside the local capacitor bank's loop, now has a fast comparator that ignores the switching spike for a few hundred nanoseconds after each turn-on. In calculation, shorts through about 100 nH of wiring or more are cut within the switches' pulse rating. A solid short at the output studs and a long-wired phase-to-negative short are not covered; both are open items. The earlier detection on the DC-link shunt saw a short late, because that shunt sits outside the local bank; it is now a slow overload channel.
      • A reverse comparator on the DC-link shunt catches phase-to-negative shorts through short-to-medium wiring. Its threshold now sits about 1.5 times above the worst normal current in a detailed model, after all tolerances; to be confirmed by measurement.
      • A logic interlock blocks a command for both switches of a leg using settled signals, checked by simulating the actual netlist with datasheet gate delays. The dead time still comes from the controller's timer, which was lengthened for hot gate thresholds.
      • The controller caps the duty cycle so the high-side gate supply always gets a recharge window. The calculated margin is small and needs measurement.
      • Field-oriented control on an STM32G474: hall sensors plus a flux observer that keeps running if the halls fail.

        170 × 110 mm. Hover a connector for what it carries.

        PDU-1 power distribution unit

        Sits between the pump battery and the DC bus of the two inverters: fuses the pack, pre-charges the bus, closes the main contactor and reports back to the engine controller.

        Placed and routed · DRC 9 violations · routing incomplete: 36 connections, including the active-discharge path, are still open · not ready to fabricate
        • The contactor cannot close until the bus has charged to 95 % of the pack voltage. The interlock is built from comparators and gates, independent of any firmware.
        • A hardware one-shot limits each pre-charge attempt and a cool-down timer blocks the next one, which bounds the energy the pre-charge resistors take into a shorted bus.
        • Two hardware over-current thresholds, one fast and one timed, open the contactor for faults downstream of it. Faults upstream of the contactor are cleared by the fuse alone.
        • The high-voltage side is isolated from the logic: isolated amplifiers for the voltages and current, isolated switch drivers for the pre-charge and discharge switches, and a 7 mm barrier with no copper on any layer.
        • 233 parts including off-board items, ERC clean. 47 of 47 sizing checks pass: pre-charge energy, resistor pulse rating, fuse and contactor coordination, copper temperature rise, spacing.

          168 × 158 mm. Hover a connector for what it carries.

          CB-1 DC-bus capacitor board, ×2

          Carries the film capacitor bank of one inverter on a laminated, heavy-copper board that bolts straight onto the inverter's DC terminals.

          Placed, planes only · DRC 0 violations, 0 unconnected · not ready to fabricate
          • Four copper layers alternate positive and negative, so the bus itself adds almost no inductance; the two bolted studs to the inverter dominate, which is why they were moved close together.
          • Analysis of the capacitor network found a resonance between the inverter's ceramic capacitors and this bank. A damper on this board would not help; it has to sit on the inverter, next to the switches.
          • Real film capacitors weigh about 2.6 times what the first estimate assumed. The full bank is the baseline; a half-populated bank is an option only together with the damper on the inverter, and then with little margin.
          • Capacitor current sharing was checked with ±20 % capacitance tolerance in a Monte Carlo run.
          • 35 parts, ERC clean. 16 of 16 checks pass.

            Finish the routing

            FC-0 has 23 connections to finish by hand. ECU-1 has none left, but its 36 V spacing, copper stack-up and long repair tracks still need a fabrication review. PDU-1 has 36, including its active-discharge path, and its logic strip needs more room or a board of its own. INV-1 has none left, but a repeat routing run on the same inputs did not converge, so the layout is not yet reproducible.

            Inverter short circuit, pulse test and ride-through

            The inverter now detects shorts inside each leg, but only in calculation: a solid short at the output studs and a long-wired phase-to-negative short are not covered, nor is a switch that has already failed short. The reverse-trip and high-side gate-supply margins need measuring, the commutation loop still needs a double-pulse test, and the new gate driver is single-sourced. The inverter's 12 V supply now has a hold-up bank that rides through an automotive-style pulse in the calculation; that has to be shown on hardware. The fault-line fix needed a resistor change on both boards, and both are now in the designs.

            Protect the rest of ECU-1

            Every ECU-1 input and the inverter discretes survive a short to the battery, and the valve outputs now trip on over-current. A valve wire shorted to ground still blows the main fuse, the trip is only analysed for harness inductance up to 2 µH, and pulse immunity on the analog sensor lines is still unchecked.

            Measure what was assumed

            Contactor arc voltage, pack resistance, regulator start-up and the watchdog's timing are datasheet extrapolations or estimates until a bench test replaces them.

            Can it be bought?

            A design that cannot be sourced is not finished. The parts lists of all the boards were merged into one vehicle set and checked against distributors and public export-control lists on 13 November 2026.

            ~300unique parts, about 1,850 placements per vehicle
            ~$1,470board components per vehicle at ten sets; design estimate about $1,315
            23 / 62critical parts checked that showed zero stock at one large distributor
            ~$1,080estimated first hardware-in-the-loop bench, a third of it still an estimate
            • For the parts priced directly, single-unit prices were a median 1.7 times the design estimate. The rest of the lines are priced with a model, not read.
            • Two parts were already obsolete: the inverter's gate driver, which has no drop-in replacement and needs a small board redesign, and a power transistor used on three boards, for which drop-in alternatives exist. By engineering judgement, about 60 of roughly 100 critical parts have at least one drop-in second source.
            • The larger practical risk is supply, not rules: several key parts come from a single maker with long factory lead times. The three microcontrollers come from one maker and showed 52-week lead times and zero stock at one distributor, while another distributor had the flight processor in stock.
            • The selected parts were also screened against public export-control lists using published classifications. The inertial sensor and the satellite-navigation receiver are the parts to watch, because a higher-grade sensor could change the classification, so any sensor change is screened before it is designed in. This is an engineering screen, not a legal determination; classifications will be confirmed with manufacturers before purchase.
            • For the first bench (one flight-computer board with fabrication and assembly, a debugger, a bench supply and an Ethernet link), the pacing items are finishing the board layout and sourcing out-of-stock chips, not the equipment. Bought-in vehicle hardware outside the boards is estimated at about $27,000 per vehicle, more than half of it an assumption that waits on supplier quotes.

            Open on the avionics

            • None of the five boards is fabrication-ready: FC-0 is routed with connections left to finish by hand, ECU-1 has no open connections but needs a spacing and stack-up review, PDU-1 has 36 connections open including its active-discharge path, INV-1 is auto-routed with every connection made, and CB-1 is placed with planes only. The details are under Still open.
            • The inverter's commutation loop must be measured with a double-pulse test and its supply ride-through confirmed; ECU-1's valve outputs still lack protection against a short to ground, and their over-current trip is an estimate until measured.
            • Contactor arc voltage, pack resistance, regulator start-up and the watchdog's timing are estimates until bench tests replace them.
