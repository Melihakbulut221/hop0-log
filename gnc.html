Guidance, navigation and control

Landing in wind

How the lander learned to land in wind up to 12 m/s once the aerodynamics came from CFD, measured after every change with the C flight software in hardware-in-the-loop (HIL).

Recorded flights

Six landings, each with a different sensor suite and wind, flown by the Python reference of the flight software (step-equivalent to the C code) in the plant with CFD aerodynamics. The whole trajectory is drawn; Replay plays it back.

All six are successful landings, picked to span the scenarios. Failures are not replayed here; they are counted in the numbers.

Wind broke the lander. The fix was in the filter.

With a simple drag model the lander made 293 of 300 in the hardest HIL scenario. CFD of the vehicle with its legs moved the centre of pressure below the centre of mass, and it fell to 159. Leg fairings and a bigger gimbal did not bring it back. One extra state in the navigation filter, then a series of guidance changes, did.

What happened

With a simple drag model, the controller landed in winds up to 12 m/s more than 96% of the time in HIL. Then that model was replaced with tables from CFD of the vehicle with its legs.

The legs pull the centre of pressure 0.15–0.5 m below the centre of mass in side flow and in descent. Falling tail first, the vehicle is aerodynamically unstable. In HIL the hardest scenario dropped from 293 to 159 landings out of 300, and even the clean COTS-sensor case fell to 75% (75 of 100).

The hardware answer, and why it didn't help

The obvious response was hardware. I designed leg fairings and a ±10° gimbal (up from ±7°) and checked both with structural analysis.

  • Fixed fairings made things worse. In crossflow they act as fins: CFD gave 2.3–2.6 times the leg side force.
  • Free-swivelling fairings passed the structural checks. But CFD showed only a 9–14% leg-force reduction in hover side wind, because about 60% of the leg force comes from parts that cannot be faired. With their extra mass (5.8 kg of parts, 6.8 kg at vehicle level with the growth allowance), the simulator landed less often, not more. They were dropped.
  • The ±10° gimbal is feasible with a re-laid-out aft bay. Its clearance sweep also found an actuator interference that already existed at ±7°. On its own it changes landing rates by almost nothing.

The filter

The navigation filter predicts the vehicle's rotation from the gimbal torque. With the CFD aerodynamics, the gimbal spends much of its authority holding the aerodynamic moment, and the filter didn't know that moment existed. It kept predicting rotation that wasn't happening, so the attitude estimate drifted exactly when control authority was tightest.

The fix is one extra state in the filter: an angular-acceleration disturbance, learnt from the gyro residual. It went into Python and C together and is tested for C/Python equivalence. Together with the ±10° gimbal, it took the hardest HIL scenario from 159 to 266 of 300. The rest came from guidance:

  • wider tilt limits near the ground, to stop the vehicle arriving at the pad still drifting;
  • a descent-speed limit that keeps the divert feasible when drag is high;
  • higher terminal-gate heights;
  • a predictive terminal law: a finite-horizon LQR that models the attitude-loop lag and plans to null position and velocity at touchdown;
  • a stiffer attitude loop at the 50 Hz flight rate, found by screening eleven candidates in the in-process HIL emulator and then confirmed in HIL on every scenario.

Every change, measured

    Step 12: the filter's model of the vehicle

    Step 12 is a correction, not a new law. When the heavier legs of CAD v0.4 went into the plant (plant v5), the same flight software fell from 299 to 275 of 300 in the hardest scenario. Changing one input at a time in the emulator showed that only the legs' moving mass mattered: returning it to its old value brought back 299. The navigation filter read its mass, inertia and centre of mass from the simulator's hull body, and since plant v4 the simulator has carried the moving part of each leg as a separate body at the foot. So the filter modelled the vehicle without those masses: too little pitch inertia, a centre of mass too high, and a gimbal torque model that predicted more angular acceleration than the vehicle had. With the lighter legs the error was small; the heavier struts exposed it. The filter now uses the mass properties of the whole vehicle, in Python and in the C code.

    What the words mean

    Landed
    A landing counts only if touchdown is below 2 m/s vertical and 1 m/s horizontal, within 6 m of the pad, and the vehicle stays upright.
    Harsh sensors
    1.5 m position noise, 1° attitude noise, 1°/s gyro noise and gyro bias.
    200 ms
    150 ms observation and 50 ms actuation latency.
    15% packet loss
    15% of the frames between simulator and flight software are bad: 10% dropped and 5% corrupted, so that their checksum fails.
    Worst aerodynamic corner
    hull drag ×1.6, leg drag ×2 against the CFD tables.
    Seed sets
    A: the seeds used for every HIL comparison since the plant was rebuilt; some earlier changes were chosen on it. B: three further blocks of 100 seeds, kept for confirmation. Screening runs use a third range of seeds, disjoint from both.
    Python, HIL, emulator
    Python: the fast simulation with the Python reference of the flight software. HIL: the C flight software as its own process over UDP. Emulator: an in-process copy of the HIL server that reproduces HIL episode by episode, used for screening.

    Trusting the right simulator

    A gain that only shows up in the fast simulator is not a gain.

    The hardest part was not the control design but knowing which result to believe. Three tools disagreed at different times: the fast Python simulation, hardware-in-the-loop, and the C code itself.

    • The fast Python loop did not model packet loss, and it twice pointed the wrong way. A filter gain it recommended made the 20 Hz HIL worst case worse (186 to 172 of 200). I wrote an in-process emulator of the HIL server, with the same random streams, latency, packet-loss and command-horizon logic. It reproduces HIL episode by episode (300 of 300 identical in the deterministic scenarios). Tuning now happens there, on seed sets disjoint from the ones used for confirmation in HIL.
    • The quick diagnostic tool ran a legacy filter mode (no accelerometer, GNSS every frame, no radar). For two changes it reported regressions that the flight configuration did not have. It now runs the flight filter by default.
    • Float32 against float64 exposed threshold races in the control law: a timer compared against a threshold, and a feed-forward that stepped on and off. Both were fixed with ramps and margins. They now cause no differences larger than rounding.

    To feel why latency and lost frames get their own rows, fly the toy landing loop: a textbook controller with generic parameters, not the flight software.

    Mistakes along the way

    Each of these produced results that looked fine and were wrong:

    • The filter modelled the vehicle without its legs. From plant v4 it read the hull body, from which the simulator takes the leg struts out; with light struts it cost one landing in 300, with the heavier redesigned struts 24. Fixed by giving the filter the whole vehicle's mass properties. Log
    • The simulator ignored the centre of mass. A MuJoCo body-frame flag kept the CoM at the hull's geometric centre, so every controller before that fix was tuned on the wrong plant. Log
    • Touchdown speed was measured after the bounce. It was read after the leg-contact impulse, so early "successful" learned policies were in fact crashing.
    • A "±10° gimbal helps" result was an artifact. The study overrode the gimbal limit in the simulator and controller but not in the navigation filter's own copy of the constant. The filter then under-modelled gimbal torque by 30%, which happened to offset the missing aerodynamic moment. Log
    • A double-counted mass. The unsprung leg masses were counted twice, which made the touchdown lateral speed 45% worse. Log
    • The same seeds in screening and confirmation. One seed set was used for both. Part of a screening gain turned out to be specific to it. Log

    Open problems

    • The worst aerodynamic-uncertainty corner (hull drag ×1.6, leg drag ×2) with 200 ms latency now lands 592 of 600 (293 and 299 on the two seed sets). An earlier version of this page said it was limited by latency rather than gains; that was wrong. Most of its failures were the filter's vehicle-model error (step 12). Failures by mean wind: 0 of 395 up to 8 m/s, 1 of 103 at 8-10, 2 of 59 at 10-11, 5 of 43 at 11-12 m/s. Until the aerodynamics is measured, the landing wind limit in this corner is 10 m/s (99.8%); the earlier 8 m/s recommendation is withdrawn.
    • Powered-descent aerodynamics, where the plume covers the base, is not modelled; the CFD descent axial force is an upper bound.
    • The two load spectra of the life twin were built on plant v4 and are being replayed on plant v5.
