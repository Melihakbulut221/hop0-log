Hop-0

A digital twin of a small reusable rocket, built before the rocket: a 1.9 m, 200 kg vertical-landing hop vehicle whose physics, aerodynamics, structure and flight code all live in software first.

Replaying recorded landings

Recorded landings at 4x speed: the Python reference of the flight software (step-equivalent to the C code) in the CFD plant. All eighteen landed; failures are counted in the tables. Vehicles drawn larger than scale.

Not built, not flown

Everything on this site happens in software. The first engine static fires, leg drop tests and tethered hops will be the twin's test: their data goes back into the plant, and the twin either agrees or gets corrected.

Four results and where they come from

Each number below states the scenario, how many runs it rests on and where its table is.

159 → 300 of 300

Landings in the hardest hardware-in-the-loop scenario

After CFD aerodynamics replaced the simple drag model the lander fell from 293 to 159; one extra state in the navigation filter, a series of guidance changes and a correction of the filter's model of the vehicle brought it back. In percent: 53% to 100%; on the held-out seeds 99.7%.

The C flight code matches its Python reference, step by step

It passes static analysis with MISRA C:2012 rules, runs fuzzing under ASan and UBSan, and gives the same outputs on a Cortex-M7 in QEMU. An in-process emulator of the HIL server reproduces HIL episode by episode.

0 of 800

Simulated leg drops inside the design envelope that tipped, bounced, bottomed or crushed a cartridge

400 drops at each of two propellant loads inside the envelope: up to 2 m/s down, 1 m/s sideways, 8° tilt, 5° slope. They are part of a 2640-drop grid that also covers harder drops outside the envelope, where the vehicle can tip. The negative finding: the legs need ground friction of 0.6 or below.

~1,400 flights

Before the first part wears out, and loss comes first

The chamber's hot wall is the first part to wear out, after a median of 1,429 flights (780 flights if film cooling is at the pessimistic end). With one loss in 600 flights in the hardest scenario, the vehicle is more likely to be lost than worn out.

How the twin is built

A twin usually mirrors a machine that already exists. This one comes first: the vehicle is designed, flown and broken in software, and only what survives gets built.

Six models run together. Each one is checked against something independent of it.

LayerModelChecked againstPage
PlantMuJoCo flight dynamics, with mass properties from CAD that change as propellant burns the CAD mass table it importsVehicle
AirRANS CFD force and moment tables from 0° to 180° angle of attack, legs included mesh-convergence and DDES checksVehicle
StructureFinite elements of tanks, legs, thrust structure and engine cooling, plus leg drop tests factors of safety 1.25 on yield, 1.4 on ultimateVehicle
GuidanceA classical controller, a multi-rate navigation filter and a predictive terminal law simulation and HIL on disjoint seed setsLanding
Flight codeC, MISRA checked the Python reference, to about 10-6; a Cortex-M7 in QEMUAvionics
LoopThe flight code as a separate process, over UDP, with latency, loss and sensor faults an in-process emulator that reproduces it episode by episodeAvionics

It is a twin in the engineering sense: the physics, the aerodynamics and the structure are modelled from the design, and the code it flies is the code the flight computer will run, not a stand-in for it.

Until there is hardware, it has nothing to sync with. Nothing on this site has flown. The twin also keeps a wear account for every simulated flight, and the same step will take real flights once there are any.
