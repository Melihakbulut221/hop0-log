Engineering log · Phase 0

Hop-0: a small reusable-rocket testbed, built in simulation first

A 1.9 m, 200 kg vertical-takeoff, vertical-landing hop vehicle. Before any hardware, every part of it lives in software: the flight dynamics, the aerodynamics, the structure, the engine controller and the flight computer's code. This page records what worked, what didn't, and how each claim was measured.

Status: simulation and hardware-in-the-loop only. The vehicle has not been built or flown.

The stack

The vehicle is a Phase-0 testbed: a 0.45 m hull, four inverted-tripod landing legs, and one 4 kN-class electric-pump liquid engine on a two-axis gimbal. The goal of Phase 0 is a controlled hop and landing. What exists today is the software and analysis around it:

  • Flight dynamics: a MuJoCo model whose mass properties come from the CAD model and change with propellant load. It includes a spring-damper leg model with a crushable honeycomb stop, a rate-limited engine and pump response from a transient engine model, gimbal backlash, and wind with gusts and shear.
  • Aerodynamics: force and moment tables from RANS CFD of the vehicle with its legs, over the full 0–180° angle-of-attack range, with mesh-convergence and DDES checks.
  • Structure: finite-element checks (CalculiX) of tanks, legs, thrust structure and engine cooling, and drop-test campaigns of the leg design.
  • Guidance, navigation and control: a classical controller with a multi-rate navigation filter (50 Hz IMU, sparse and delayed GNSS, radar altimeter).
  • Flight software: a C port that matches the Python reference to about 10-6 step by step. It passes static analysis (gcc analyzer, clang-tidy, cppcheck with MISRA C:2012), runs fuzzing under ASan and UBSan, and runs on a Cortex-M7 target in QEMU with the same outputs.
  • Hardware-in-the-loop: the C flight software runs as a separate process and talks to the simulator over a UDP protocol with timestamps, CRC, injected latency, packet loss and sensor faults.

Wind broke the lander

With a simple drag model, the controller landed in winds up to 12 m/s more than 96% of the time in HIL. Then that model was replaced with tables from CFD of the vehicle with its legs.

The legs pull the centre of pressure 0.15–0.5 m below the centre of mass in side flow and in descent. Falling tail first, the vehicle is aerodynamically unstable. In HIL the hardest scenario dropped from 293 to 159 landings out of 300, and even the clean COTS-sensor case fell to 75%.

The hardware answer, and why it didn't help

The obvious response was hardware. I designed leg fairings and a ±10° gimbal (up from ±7°) and checked both with structural analysis.

  • Fixed fairings made things worse. In crossflow they act as fins: CFD gave 2.3–2.6 times the leg side force.
  • Free-swivelling fairings passed the structural checks. But CFD showed only a 9–14% leg-force reduction in hover side wind, because about 60% of the leg force comes from parts that cannot be faired. With their 5.8 kg of extra mass, the simulator landed less often, not more. They were dropped.
  • The ±10° gimbal is feasible with a re-laid-out aft bay. Its clearance sweep also found an actuator interference that already existed at ±7°. On its own it changes landing rates by almost nothing.

The fix was in the filter, not the hardware

The navigation filter predicts the vehicle's rotation from the gimbal torque. With the CFD aerodynamics, the gimbal spends much of its authority holding the aerodynamic moment, and the filter didn't know that moment existed. It kept predicting rotation that wasn't happening, so the attitude estimate drifted exactly when control authority was tightest.

The fix is one extra state in the filter: an angular-acceleration disturbance, learnt from the gyro residual. It went into Python and C together and is tested for C/Python equivalence. Together with the ±10° gimbal (which on its own changes little), it took the hardest HIL scenario from 159 to 266 of 300.

The rest of the recovery came from guidance, each step measured in the same HIL scenario:

  • wider tilt limits near the ground, to stop the vehicle arriving at the pad still drifting;
  • a descent-speed limit that keeps the divert feasible when drag is high;
  • higher terminal-gate heights;
  • a predictive terminal law: a finite-horizon LQR that models the attitude-loop lag and plans to null position and velocity at touchdown.

Trusting the right simulator

The hardest part was not the control design but knowing which result to believe. Three tools disagreed at different times: the fast Python simulation, hardware-in-the-loop, and the C code itself.

  • The fast Python loop did not model packet loss, and it twice pointed the wrong way. A filter gain it recommended made the 20 Hz HIL worst case worse (186 to 172 of 200). I wrote an in-process emulator of the HIL server, with the same random streams, latency, packet-loss and command-horizon logic. It reproduces HIL episode by episode (300 of 300 identical in the deterministic scenarios). Tuning now happens there, on seed sets disjoint from the ones used for confirmation in HIL.
  • The quick diagnostic tool ran a legacy filter mode (no accelerometer, GNSS every frame, no radar). For two changes it reported regressions that the flight configuration did not have. It now runs the flight filter by default.
  • Float32 against float64 exposed threshold races in the control law: a timer compared against 1.0 s, and a feed-forward that stepped on and off. Both were fixed with ramps and margins. They now cause no differences larger than rounding.

Mistakes along the way

Each of these produced results that looked fine and were wrong:

  • The simulator ignored the centre of mass. A MuJoCo body-frame flag kept the CoM at the hull's geometric centre, so every controller before that fix was tuned on the wrong plant.
  • Touchdown speed was measured after the bounce. It was read after the leg-contact impulse, so early "successful" learned policies were in fact crashing.
  • A "±10° gimbal helps" result was an artifact. The study overrode the gimbal limit in the simulator and controller but not in the navigation filter's own copy of the constant. The filter then under-modelled gimbal torque by 30%, which happened to offset the missing aerodynamic moment.
  • A double-counted mass. The unsprung leg masses were counted twice, which made the touchdown lateral speed 45% worse.
  • The same seeds in screening and confirmation. One seed set was used for both. Part of a screening gain turned out to be specific to it.

Open problems

  • The worst aerodynamic-uncertainty corner (hull drag ×1.6, leg drag ×2) with 200 ms latency is still about 93%. It is limited by latency rather than gains.
  • Powered-descent aerodynamics, where the plume covers the base, is not modelled; the CFD descent axial force is an upper bound.
  • The flight-computer and engine-controller boards are schematic-complete but their PCBs are not fabrication-ready, and the power-distribution unit is not designed.
  • The cooling design depends on a film-cooling effectiveness that has to be measured before a regeneratively cooled chamber is built.
