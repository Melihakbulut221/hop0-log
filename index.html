A reusable rocket that lands before it exists

Recorded landings at 4x speed. Vehicles drawn larger than scale.

Hop-0 is a digital twin of a small reusable rocket, built before the rocket: a 1.9 m, 200 kg vertical-landing hop vehicle whose physics, aerodynamics, structure and flight code all live in software first.

In the hardest hardware-in-the-loop scenario it now lands 99.7% of the time, up from 53%, on the same 300 random seeds.

The whole vehicle

1.9 m, five bays

Nose, forward avionics bay, two propellant tanks with an intertank between them, and an aft bay that holds the engine, pumps, power electronics and the legs. About 90 kg dry by the CAD estimate, about 200 kg fuelled.

The tanks are the hull: there is no outer shell around them.

01 · Nose and pressurant

Radome and helium

A glass-fibre radome keeps the GNSS antenna and the telemetry link radio-transparent. Under it, a helium composite overwrapped pressure vessel sits on its own ring frame, held at its equator by a band and four struts.

Its regulator and latch valve pressurise both tanks.

02 · Avionics deck

The flight computer's floor

A ribbed aluminium deck carries the flight computer in its enclosure, the RTK GNSS receiver, the telemetry radio and the flight termination unit, which has its own battery. The IMU and the avionics battery hang underneath.

The ribs are there for stiffness: the deck's first structural mode is above 160 Hz.

03 · Tanks

Two tanks that are also the airframe

RP-1 above, liquid oxygen below, in separate aluminium tanks with elliptical domes. Y-rings carry the load from the skirts into the tank walls at each weld. A ring baffle in each tank damps slosh at the fill level the vehicle lands with.

The intertank carries the pressurisation lines; an external raceway takes the RP-1 feed and the harness down past the oxygen tank.

04 · Feed

Electric pumps

Each propellant has its own pump: a brushless motor under a volute, a pre-valve on the inlet and a main valve on the outlet. Flexible hoses with a loop let the engine gimbal without loading the lines.

Fill and drain valves end in quick-disconnects for the ground side.

05 · Engine

Chamber, injector, gimbal

A regeneratively cooled chamber: the fuel runs through channels in the wall before it is injected. A pintle injector, a spark-torch igniter, and a cardan joint under the thrust structure.

Two electromechanical actuators push on a ring around the chamber and swing the engine ±10° in pitch and yaw. A clearance sweep over every corner of that range checks that nothing touches.

06 · Power

A battery that drives the pumps

A LiPo pack feeds the two pump inverters, one per pump, through a power-distribution unit with a pre-charge resistor and a contactor that the engine controller switches.

The radar altimeter looks straight down from the aft bay.

07 · Structure

Where the loads go

The aft skirt carries everything below the tanks. Crossed box beams and a centre plate take the engine's thrust and the gimbal loads into a thrust ring, and from there into the frame that the legs attach to.

Doubler plates spread the leg loads where the struts meet the skin; a base frame closes the bottom.

08 · Legs

Four inverted tripods

Each leg has a primary strut with a coil-over shock and a crushable honeycomb cartridge behind it, two secondary struts, and a foot. Clevises attach the struts to the body; rod ends close the strut tubes.

A switch in each foot tells the flight computer it is on the ground. The leg design went through drop-test campaigns in simulation before it went into the flight model.

Mechanical
design

The vehicle exists as code before it exists as metal. Every part is built from the design files, weighed, checked for clashes and handed to the simulator. Then the structure, the legs and the gimbal are checked against the loads they will see.

01 · Parametric model

Every part comes from a number

The CAD model is a CadQuery program. Hull length and diameter, tank volumes, wall thicknesses, the leg geometry and the engine envelope are read from the same JSON files that the sizing scripts and the simulator use. Change an input, and the model, its mass properties and a mass table for the simulator are rebuilt together; a separate import step brings that table into the flight dynamics.

Parts that are actually designed here (tanks, structure, legs, lines) get their mass from volume times density. Bought-in black boxes such as pumps, valves, batteries, inverters, actuators and avionics enter as envelopes carrying their budget or catalogue mass.

02 · Mass

89.8 kg dry, 4.4% over budget

The model's current best estimate against the Phase-0 mass budget of 86.0 kg. With the 20% growth allowance that is 107.7 kg. The simulator currently flies 107.3 kg, imported from the previous CAD revision. Hover a bar for what it contains.

The largest overrun is the airframe, at 40% over. Its skin thicknesses are still placeholders that no structural optimisation has touched yet. The landing legs come in 16% over budget with the original strut walls; the thicker walls that a later check asks for would add up to 2.6 kg more.

Valves, lines and roll control look light only because the model holds the discrete parts and none of the brackets, fittings and plumbing. Their budget stays. Avionics is under budget for the same reason: no cable trays or brackets. The leg fairings studied for the wind problem were dropped and are not in these numbers.

03 · Mass properties

The centre of mass climbs as the tanks fill

Both tanks sit above the dry centre of mass, liquid oxygen below RP-1. Loading them lifts the centre of mass by 14 cm and adds over a quarter to the pitch inertia. A table like this, imported into the simulator, is interpolated as propellant burns. Drag the slider or hover a chart.

04 · Structure

What the finite-element models checked

CalculiX models of the tanks, the whole hull as shells and beams, the thrust structure and the legs, loaded with full-thrust flight cases, the drop-test envelope and the leg's load limit, tanks pressurised. Each bar is the lowest margin of safety found for that item: zero means the part just meets its factor of safety (1.25 on yield, 1.4 on ultimate).

Open

One margin is negative and still open. In the two hardest landing cases, the free upper edge of the forward hull, where the nose joins, reaches −0.24 and −0.28. The vehicle's yaw and side acceleration push the forward-bay masses into a thin edge that nothing supports. The hull model has no stiffening ring at the nose joint yet; the helium vessel's ring frame just below it is in the model only as a mass. Adding a stiff ring at the joint is the planned fix, and it has not been analysed.

Before the leg redesign, the base frame and a tank weld showed negative margins too. A thicker base frame, doubler plates and the tripod legs brought every landing case on the leg load path and the tanks to +0.30 or better. Buckling does not drive the design: the first mode is in the intertank, at +6.8 after knock-down. The tank, buckling and hull-mode results come from the first full-hull analysis; the tank geometry has not changed since. In that earlier model the hull's own first bending mode was about 100 Hz.

05 · Landing legs

A tripod that turns side loads into axial ones

Each leg is three pinned struts meeting at the foot. Any force on the foot splits into three axial strut forces, so the struts carry mostly axial load. The first design, a truss with a vertical shock at its tip, could not carry foot friction at all: the shock became a cantilever.

A coil-over shock absorbs a normal landing. Behind it, a crushable honeycomb cartridge caps the strut force if the landing is worse than designed for. The shock damps far more on rebound than on compression, so the vehicle does not bounce.

2640simulated drops: 1320 at each of two propellant loads
0tip-overs, bounces, bottomed shocks or crushed cartridges inside the envelope
≤ 0.6ground friction the legs need

The negative finding is friction. As the leg compresses, the foot slides outward, and friction resists that slide, so the leg gets stiffer as the ground gets grippier. Peak deceleration inside the envelope rises from 5–8 g to 17.5 g at a friction coefficient of 1, and the leg would lock solid at 1.6. The landing surface therefore has to keep friction at 0.6 or below: a low-friction foot sole or a steel landing plate.

Open: a later check found what the first leg sizing had missed. The sizing left out the struts' own sideways inertia, which the drop model shows is large near the foot, and the drop model underestimated the leg's swinging mass. With both, the original struts' lowest margin of +0.09 no longer holds and the walls have to get thicker; how much, without fairings, is still open. And the primary strut's first bending mode is about 65 Hz, not the 140–150 Hz first estimated, close to the 50 Hz control loop. Command smoothing, a notch filter and a modal test are on the list.

06 · Avionics deck

From 27 Hz to 163 Hz

The first deck was a flat plate under about 15 kg of equipment. On it, the helium vessel rocked at 27 Hz, right where a 50 Hz control loop folds its noise. With ribs it still rocked at 49 Hz on its mounting ring.

Four light struts from the vessel's equator to its own ring frame in the forward hull take that load straight into the skin. The first mode is now 163 Hz, and it belongs to the helium regulator, not the deck.

07 · Gimbal clearance

Swinging the engine through every corner

The sweep moves the engine group through the ±10° range in both axes, corners included, where the total tilt is 14.1°. The actuators move with it. At each of 32 attitudes, every moving part is checked against every fixed one using point clouds and a k-d tree.

        PDU-1 power distribution unit

        Placed and routed · DRC 9 violations · routing incomplete: 36 connections, including the active-discharge path, are still open · not ready to fabricate

            Loss, not wear, sets the life

            The first part to wear out is the chamber's hot wall, after about 1,400 flights, 780 if film cooling turns out at the pessimistic end. That is twenty times the engine's requirement. But at one loss in 600 flights in this scenario, the vehicle is more likely to be lost than worn out. Reliability is where the work goes next.

            What the numbers rest on

            Leg loads come from replaying each HIL flight at the physics rate and checking it against the HIL log, episode by episode. The battery cycle life, the weld fatigue class and the shock service interval are assumptions until a datasheet or a test replaces them; the twin says which.

            A bug it found

            Building the twin exposed a sign error in the simulator's honeycomb limiter: past its crush point it stiffened the leg instead of capping the force. No landing in these records reached that point, so no result changes, but it would have mattered for crash studies.

            Next: real data

            The same step that adds a simulated flight will add a real one, from telemetry and a stroke sensor on each leg. The first static fires and drop tests will replace the assumptions one by one.

            Every change,
            measured

            The hardest scenario (harsh sensors, 200 ms latency, 15% packet loss, wind up to 12 m/s), the same 300 episodes after every step. Changes that did not hold up in HIL were reverted and are not shown.

            The stack

            The vehicle is a Phase-0 testbed: a 0.45 m hull, four inverted-tripod landing legs, and one 4 kN-class electric-pump liquid engine on a two-axis gimbal. The goal of Phase 0 is a controlled hop and landing.

            • Flight dynamics: a MuJoCo model whose mass properties come from the CAD model and change with propellant load. It includes a spring-damper leg model with a crushable honeycomb stop, a rate-limited engine and pump response from a transient engine model, gimbal backlash, and wind with gusts and shear.
            • Aerodynamics: force and moment tables from RANS CFD of the vehicle with its legs, over the full 0–180° angle-of-attack range, with mesh-convergence and DDES checks.
            • Structure: finite-element checks (CalculiX) of tanks, legs, thrust structure and engine cooling, and drop-test campaigns of the leg design.
            • Guidance, navigation and control: a classical controller with a multi-rate navigation filter (50 Hz IMU, sparse and delayed GNSS, radar altimeter).
            • Flight software: a C port that matches the Python reference to about 10-6 step by step. It passes static analysis (gcc analyzer, clang-tidy, cppcheck with MISRA C:2012), runs fuzzing under ASan and UBSan, and runs on a Cortex-M7 target in QEMU with the same outputs.
            • Hardware-in-the-loop: the C flight software runs as a separate process and talks to the simulator over a UDP protocol with timestamps, CRC, injected latency, packet loss and sensor faults.

            Wind broke the lander

            With a simple drag model, the controller landed in winds up to 12 m/s more than 96% of the time in HIL. Then that model was replaced with tables from CFD of the vehicle with its legs.

            The legs pull the centre of pressure 0.15–0.5 m below the centre of mass in side flow and in descent. Falling tail first, the vehicle is aerodynamically unstable. In HIL the hardest scenario dropped from 293 to 159 landings out of 300, and even the clean COTS-sensor case fell to 75%.

            The hardware answer, and why it didn't help

            The obvious response was hardware. I designed leg fairings and a ±10° gimbal (up from ±7°) and checked both with structural analysis.

            • Fixed fairings made things worse. In crossflow they act as fins: CFD gave 2.3–2.6 times the leg side force.
            • Free-swivelling fairings passed the structural checks. But CFD showed only a 9–14% leg-force reduction in hover side wind, because about 60% of the leg force comes from parts that cannot be faired. With their 5.8 kg of extra mass, the simulator landed less often, not more. They were dropped.
            • The ±10° gimbal is feasible with a re-laid-out aft bay. Its clearance sweep also found an actuator interference that already existed at ±7°. On its own it changes landing rates by almost nothing.

            The filter

            The navigation filter predicts the vehicle's rotation from the gimbal torque. With the CFD aerodynamics, the gimbal spends much of its authority holding the aerodynamic moment, and the filter didn't know that moment existed. It kept predicting rotation that wasn't happening, so the attitude estimate drifted exactly when control authority was tightest.

            The fix is one extra state in the filter: an angular-acceleration disturbance, learnt from the gyro residual. It went into Python and C together and is tested for C/Python equivalence. Together with the ±10° gimbal, it took the hardest HIL scenario from 159 to 266 of 300. The rest came from guidance:

            • wider tilt limits near the ground, to stop the vehicle arriving at the pad still drifting;
            • a descent-speed limit that keeps the divert feasible when drag is high;
            • higher terminal-gate heights;
            • a predictive terminal law: a finite-horizon LQR that models the attitude-loop lag and plans to null position and velocity at touchdown;
            • a stiffer attitude loop at the 50 Hz flight rate, found by screening twelve candidates in the in-process HIL emulator and then confirmed in HIL on every scenario.

            Trusting the right simulator

            The hardest part was not the control design but knowing which result to believe. Three tools disagreed at different times: the fast Python simulation, hardware-in-the-loop, and the C code itself.

            • The fast Python loop did not model packet loss, and it twice pointed the wrong way. A filter gain it recommended made the 20 Hz HIL worst case worse (186 to 172 of 200). I wrote an in-process emulator of the HIL server, with the same random streams, latency, packet-loss and command-horizon logic. It reproduces HIL episode by episode (300 of 300 identical in the deterministic scenarios). Tuning now happens there, on seed sets disjoint from the ones used for confirmation in HIL.
            • The quick diagnostic tool ran a legacy filter mode (no accelerometer, GNSS every frame, no radar). For two changes it reported regressions that the flight configuration did not have. It now runs the flight filter by default.
            • Float32 against float64 exposed threshold races in the control law: a timer compared against 1.0 s, and a feed-forward that stepped on and off. Both were fixed with ramps and margins. They now cause no differences larger than rounding.

            Mistakes along the way

            Each of these produced results that looked fine and were wrong:

            • The simulator ignored the centre of mass. A MuJoCo body-frame flag kept the CoM at the hull's geometric centre, so every controller before that fix was tuned on the wrong plant.
            • Touchdown speed was measured after the bounce. It was read after the leg-contact impulse, so early "successful" learned policies were in fact crashing.
            • A "±10° gimbal helps" result was an artifact. The study overrode the gimbal limit in the simulator and controller but not in the navigation filter's own copy of the constant. The filter then under-modelled gimbal torque by 30%, which happened to offset the missing aerodynamic moment.
            • A double-counted mass. The unsprung leg masses were counted twice, which made the touchdown lateral speed 45% worse.
            • The same seeds in screening and confirmation. One seed set was used for both. Part of a screening gain turned out to be specific to it.

            Open problems

            • The worst aerodynamic-uncertainty corner (hull drag ×1.6, leg drag ×2) with 200 ms latency is now about 96% (574 of 600), up from 94% with a higher attitude-loop gain. It is limited by latency rather than gains.
            • Powered-descent aerodynamics, where the plume covers the base, is not modelled; the CFD descent axial force is an upper bound.
            • The flight-computer and engine-controller boards are schematic-complete but their PCBs are not fabrication-ready, and the power-distribution unit is not designed.
            • The cooling design depends on a film-cooling effectiveness that has to be measured before a regeneratively cooled chamber is built.

            • Shrinking the payload from 5 kg to 1 kg saves only about a quarter of the liftoff mass: avionics, fairing and minimum-gauge structure set a floor of about two tonnes.
            • The smallest vehicle that reaches orbit is a demonstrator, not a product: its cost per flight is several times what the market pays for a few kilograms.
            • Cost per kilogram falls by more than an order of magnitude between 5 kg and 150 kg of payload; only around the 300 kg class does the estimated cost per flight fall below what dedicated small launches sell for today, which sets the size of the first commercial product.
            • Engine efficiency is the largest single lever: about 5 % more specific impulse saves roughly a sixth of the liftoff mass.
            • The simulator is consistent with a flown three-stage micro launcher (a calibrated check: some inputs of that vehicle are not public and were assumed) and over-predicts a flown two-stage launcher by about 15 %; the sizing carries an extra margin for that bias. Comparable launchers under development quote 10-25 % less payload for a similar liftoff mass, so the curve is on the optimistic side of its peers.

            What “digital twin”
            means here

            A twin usually mirrors a machine that already exists. This one comes first: the vehicle is designed, flown and broken in software, and only what survives gets built.

            It is a twin in the engineering sense: the physics, the aerodynamics and the structure are modelled from the design, and the code it flies is the code the flight computer will run, not a stand-in for it.

            Until there is hardware, it has nothing to sync with. The first engine static fires, leg drop tests and tethered hops will be its test: their data goes back into the plant, and the twin either agrees or gets corrected. Nothing on this page has flown.
