Wear account

The twin keeps a wear account. Every flight record is turned into damage for each part that wears out: the chamber's hot wall, the pump battery, the shocks, the honeycomb, the struts, the welded hull zones and the gimbal actuators. The flights here are 600 hardware-in-the-loop flights in the hardest scenario, flown by the flight software one revision before its latest attitude-gain change, so this is a cautious load spectrum.

Wear barely depends on the scenario mix: every hop heats the chamber and drains the battery about the same, and landing loads stay well inside the leg's envelope. Loss was the open question. A campaign of 3300 more HIL flights on new seeds (2026-11-12) had no loss either: with 4400 loss-free flights the loss rate is below 0.087% per flight with 95% confidence. Eight flights in that campaign were hard landings, all on sideways speed at 1.0 to 1.9 m/s, seven of them in winds near 10 m/s or more.

Plant v5 (2026-11-10) carries the heavier CAD v0.4 legs, whose moving mass per leg went from 1.32 to 1.95 kg. Its column is the same eleven-scenario regression replayed on plant v5; all 1100 landings matched the HIL log. Wear is unchanged; the heavier feet raise the peak landing load a little, but the landings are cleaner, so the shock uses at most 37% of its stroke instead of 71%. The console above still flies the cautious plant v4 spectrum. Log.

Loss and wear, now the same order

The first part to wear out is the chamber's hot wall, after about 1,400 flights, 780 if film cooling turns out at the pessimistic end. That is twenty times the engine's requirement. With the earlier software in the hardest scenario, one loss in 600 flights made loss come first. With the current software no loss in 4400 flights bounds the median flights before a loss above about 800: the same order as wear, and only a bound, not a measurement.

What the numbers rest on

Leg loads come from replaying each HIL flight at the physics rate and checking it against the HIL log, episode by episode. The battery cycle life, the weld fatigue class and the shock service interval are assumptions until a datasheet or a test replaces them; the twin says which.

A bug it found

Building the twin exposed a sign error in the simulator's honeycomb limiter: past its crush point it stiffened the leg instead of capping the force. No landing in these records reached that point, so no result changes, but it would have mattered for crash studies.

Next: real data

The same step that adds a simulated flight will add a real one, from telemetry and a stroke sensor on each leg. The first static fires and drop tests will replace the assumptions one by one.
