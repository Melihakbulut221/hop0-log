Payload against liftoff mass

Hop-0 is the first step toward a small launcher. How small can a rocket be and still reach orbit, and which size is worth building? Every design below was flown to orbit in a 3-DOF ascent simulation with its own optimised steering before it counted, and carries a performance reserve.

  • Shrinking the payload from 5 kg to 1 kg saves only about a quarter of the liftoff mass: avionics, fairing and minimum-gauge structure set a floor of about two tonnes.
  • The smallest vehicle that reaches orbit is a demonstrator, not a product: its cost per flight is several times what the market pays for a few kilograms.
  • Cost per kilogram falls by more than an order of magnitude between 5 kg and 150 kg of payload; only around the 300 kg class does the estimated cost per flight fall below what dedicated small launches sell for today, which sets the size of the first commercial product.
  • Engine efficiency is the largest single lever: about 5 % more specific impulse saves roughly a sixth of the liftoff mass.
  • The simulator is consistent with a flown three-stage micro launcher (a calibrated check: some inputs of that vehicle are not public and were assumed) and over-predicts a flown two-stage launcher by about 15 %; the sizing carries an extra margin for that bias. Comparable launchers under development quote 10-25 % less payload for a similar liftoff mass, so the curve is on the optimistic side of its peers.

Minimum within the studied architecture: first-stage thrust-to-weight, engine count and nozzles were not traded. Expendable vehicles; composite-class structure. A study, not a vehicle design.

Does landing the first stage pay?

Each recovery option was re-sized with the same ascent simulator and optimiser as the expendable baseline, for the same 300 kg to a 500 km circular orbit, and costed with the same model.

"Ideal" assumes no booster losses and no reuse overheads. "Central" adds three costs that will occur: lost production learning when far fewer boosters are built, the amortised development of recovery, and booster losses during commissioning. Cost changes are relative to the expendable vehicle with the same payload.

Recommendation: size the first orbital product for propulsive landing on a ship and fly its first missions expendable. The same vehicle carries about a fifth more when expended, about 350-370 kg, so it can earn revenue while the early flights carry the usual maiden-flight risk. Return to the launch site is not recommended for this class.

  • The first stage is more than half of the cost of a flight here, so recovery pays back after two to three flights per booster; for a few-kilogram launcher it would not.
  • Re-entry heating stays just under the limit used for operational boosters, so no braking burn is needed for heat. The aerodynamic load on the way down probably does need one; a conservative design with a braking burn is about 5% heavier and keeps most of the saving.
  • The landing needs the engine to throttle deeper than the ascent nozzle allows. The planned deep-throttle range of the Hop-0 engine family is what gives margin below hover for a precise touchdown; that is one of the things Hop-0 has to demonstrate.
  • Every landing needs all landing engines to restart, so restart reliability sets a floor on the loss rate. The economics tolerate about one loss in twelve landings; the operational target is set by pad damage, production cadence and restart reliability instead.
  • The saving is fragile: combining pessimistic refurbishment, recovery operations, learning-curve, development and booster-life assumptions erases it. A recovery kit half again as heavy would make the vehicle at least a tenth heavier. The booster descent is a point-mass simulation without aerodynamic control or structural loads.
